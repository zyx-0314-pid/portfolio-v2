Teaching Data Structures as Decisions, Not Definitions

How I used real system problems, adversarial testing, and oral defense to push students beyond memorizing data structures and toward choosing them for a reason.

Context
FEU Institute of Technology
Subject
Data Structures & Algorithms
Teaching Method
Build · Break · Defend

August 11, 2026

01 / Why I Taught It This Way

A Working System Before a Definition

I wanted students to see why data structures mattered inside systems they could imagine, not only through isolated exercises. Conventional DSA learning could become theoretical and memorization-heavy: students could identify a structure but still hesitate when asked when to use it.

Decision-making was the missing step. Arrays became the answer to almost every collection, and at the worst extreme, a separate variable held every value. Changing collections of data were difficult to model because the structure had been chosen before the behavior was understood.

The C++ Employee Management System sample connected OOP, linked structures, nested data, applicant-to-employee data movement, CRUD, and separation of responsibilities inside one working example.

The sample was not the lesson itself. It was supporting evidence that Data Structures and Algorithms could participate in an actual system.

Knowing the name of a data structure is not the same as knowing when to use it.

02 / Before They Could Code

Ask About the Data First

Before choosing a data structure, understand what the data is expected to do.

  • What are we actually dealing with?
  • Is this data required or optional?
  • Will there be more than one?
  • Is the amount fixed, bounded, or able to grow?
  • Can something leave from the middle?
  • Does order matter?
  • Should it behave like FIFO?
  • Should it behave like FILO?
  • Should it remain sorted?
  • Could it contain a large number of values?
  • Do we already know what we are searching for?
  • Can the values be sorted before insertion?
  • Are the keys numeric, character-based, or alphanumeric?
  • How frequently will data be inserted?
  • How frequently will it be removed?
  • How frequently will it be searched?

These questions did not form a rigid algorithm that always produced one correct structure. They reduced blind choices and made assumptions visible.

System constraints→Data behavior→Candidate structures→Tradeoffs→Decision

03 / Their Project Was Their Problem

One Topic, One System, Their Decisions

Students did not modify the Employee Management System. Each student selected a domain and designed a solution around that system's own data and behavior. The sample could demonstrate a way of thinking, but it could not decide their constraints for them.

RORO ticketing

Capacity, passenger records, schedules, and movement.

Bus ticketing

Routes, seats, queues, and cancellations.

Movie ticketing

Showtimes, reservations, seats, and availability.

Hospital queue

Arrival, priority, service order, and discharge.

Store

Inventory, transactions, lookup, and removal.

Delivery service

Packages, assignments, status, and sequence.

Mail service

Intake, routing, recipients, and delivery state.

Other systems

Any suitable domain with defensible data behavior.

The assignment changed the question from “What data structure did Sir use?” to “What does my system need?”

04 / There Was Not Always One Correct Answer

Defend the Tradeoff

Different developers could choose different structures and both solutions could remain acceptable when the reasoning survived examination.

Known ferry capacity

If the maximum passengers or items were already known, selecting a dynamically expanding linked structure automatically was questionable. A fixed-size array or array/object model could be a credible candidate.

Questions the choice triggered

  • Why a linked list?
  • Is dynamic growth required?
  • Which operation benefits?
  • What caveat did the choice introduce?
  • What alternative was considered?

I accepted caveats when the student understood the restriction and had a plan for it. Software engineering often permits several workable solutions; the goal was not to guess which one I preferred, but to present one that survived questioning.

A limitation can be acceptable. An unexplained limitation is not.

05 / The Rubric

Working Code Was Only the First Hurdle

Functional CRUD

20 points

The functionality students promised had to work. CRUD was the first hurdle; a clever DSA choice did not compensate for broken required behavior.

Data Structure + Algorithm

Maximum 20 points

Required at least one Data Structure and one Algorithm.

  • 30% applied or implemented
  • 70% defended through reasoning

Additional structures or algorithms could contribute bonus scoring according to the grading approach. The important distinction was that defense carried more weight than merely containing a structure in code.

Code Base

30 points · Start at 30

Every identified code-quality mistake deducted one point. It was deliberately a number game: small and large mistakes counted as individual deductions.

  • Poor naming
  • DRY violations
  • GOTO usage
  • Misunderstanding intended behavior
  • Weak structure
  • Code the student could not explain
  • AI-generated implementation without understanding

Error Handling / Breaking Tests

20 points

Four errors or failure situations had to be caught gracefully. Each was worth five points. A situation could be introduced by the student or by me during evaluation.

Bonus

Up to 10 points

Bonus scoring remained capped at ten points; I am not assigning a more detailed formula here than the grading approach supports.

06 / I Tried to Break Their Systems

If It Only Works on the Happy Path, It Does Not Work Yet

System-breaking was repeated classroom practice, not a final surprise. I introduced situational problems based on the system each student had built, and they were expected to handle four failure situations gracefully.

Invalid state, unexpected input, impossible operations, missing data, unusual operation order, and boundary conditions describe the kinds of failure categories involved. They were not six fixed universal tests.

Build↓Test↓Break↓Handle↓Defend↓Revise

Repeated attempts to break the program trained students to anticipate failure before submission.

07 / A Student Challenged the Obvious Choice

The Answer Wasn't the Linked List. The Reasoning Was.

A student used a linked structure in a distribution system even though the amount to distribute was initially known. At first, a fixed-size collection appeared possible, so the linked structure needed a reason beyond familiarity.

Her reasoning focused on the lifecycle of the records. Claimers would come and go, claimed entries would leave the active collection, the active set would progressively become smaller, and later searches would operate against the remaining active records. This did not make a linked list universally faster than an array, nor prove it was objectively the fastest design. The value was in modeling how the active dataset changed over time and defending the structure around that lifecycle.

Known initial set→Claim→Remove active record→Smaller active set→Search remaining records

The design also combined DSA with OOP. Characters or entities shared a common base structure while allowing individual data to expand where needed. She was no longer asking which structure had been memorized for the problem. She was asking what happened to the data while the system was running.

The answer was less important than the model she built in her head before coding.

08 / AI Made Defense More Important

If You Cannot Defend It, You Do Not Own It

Explanation after the code

Some students relied heavily on AI-generated implementation and could ask AI to explain it afterward. At the surface, they could repeat that explanation.

Reasoning under changed conditions

Deeper situational questions exposed whether they understood why the structure was chosen, which alternative existed, what changed under new constraints, which failure appeared, and what tradeoff they had accepted.

This is not an argument against AI use. AI made oral defense and reasoning checks more important. The issue was depending on code that the student could not reason about.

An explanation generated after the code is not the same as a decision made before the code.

09 / What Changed

They Started Asking Better Questions Than Mine

Observed outcomes, not measured KPIs

  • Students asked better questions before coding.
  • They considered situations beyond the questions I provided.
  • They showed stronger awareness of constraints.
  • They were more willing to compare multiple solutions.
  • They reasoned more carefully about how data changes.
  • They paid more attention to failure cases.
  • They became more able to defend compromises.
  • They understood that acceptable caveats still required a plan.

When I encountered some of these students again in later Database subjects, I observed stronger reasoning in how they approached data and systems. That is a personal teaching observation, not a measured longitudinal result or a claim that this method caused the later behavior.

Some of the strongest interactions happened when students challenged the expected solution and the discussion approached practical limits of the language or system. That was acceptable. The goal was not obedience to my answer; it was defensible engineering reasoning.

10 / Closing

DSA Is Application

What I value most is not whether students memorized every structure or algorithm. It is the quality of their decisions when building systems and managing data.

Data structures→Constraints→System behavior→Algorithms→Tradeoffs→Decisions
The moment DSA becomes useful is when the student stops asking “What structure am I supposed to use?” and starts asking “What does this data need to do?”
